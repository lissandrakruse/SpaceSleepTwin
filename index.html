LIGHT INPUT

Timing matters

Lux values are photopic. Melanopic EDI cannot be substituted into this model without validating a spectral response. Ambient measurements do not reconstruct personal exposure. The photopic model does not represent the dynamic spectrum; whether this omission underestimates the physiological contrast is untested. Horizontal melanopic and vertical photopic measurements should not be compared as a matched spectral ratio.

WHAT THE TEST SUPPORTS

Evidence, assumptions and next questions

Published observation

The HERA aggregate acrophases were 6.22 h under standard and 4.76 h under dynamic light. This is a terrestrial analog result, not an in-flight dataset.

Exploratory reconstruction

Matched initial states, 21 assumed premission days, two 8-hour and five 5-hour sleep opportunities per cycle. Light is summarized by one habitat level; sleep exposure is zero.

Still to test

Link the model phase proxy to the measured marker, audit the full light protocol and evaluate independent individual records. Prediction and intervention benefit require separate tests.

Changing the weekly cycle offset barely changed the default contrast (−1.335 to −1.327 h). Changing the intrinsic period to 23.8 or 24.6 h yielded −0.690 or approximately −1.142 h at the finer browser time resolution: physiological assumptions matter. These scenarios are not confidence intervals.

SCIENTIFIC ROBUSTNESS

One close match is not enough

Fixed scenarios repeat the notebook conditions. The browser uses 30-second steps. At τ=24.6 h, refining the Python output grid from 5 minutes to 30 seconds changes the contrast from −1.133 to −1.142 h, matching the browser. These scenarios are not confidence intervals, fitted individuals or extra participants.

Baseline phase matters

The default discrepancy of about 8 minutes applies to the unadjusted aggregate mission contrast. It is not a causal effect estimate or proof of individual accuracy.

NEXT SCIENTIFIC TEST

From reconstruction to independent evaluation

1 · Calibrate

Estimate the person's starting phase and history from prior observations. Freeze model settings and establish a validated link between phase markers.

2 · Predict

Evaluate unseen people or future nights against personal-history and phase baselines. Report participant-level errors and uncertainty, including failures.

3 · Intervene

Test model-guided lighting against a defined control. Measured improvement requires intervention evidence; favorable simulated phase does not prove better sleep.

Original contribution proposed: an auditable integration and evaluation framework. The HERA lighting finding is the original authors' result. The current software is a reproducible exploratory benchmark, not a confirmed discovery.

Daytime boost or evening dimming?

Four model schedules share the same starting state and sleep timing. At Level 1 and τ = 24.2 h, dimming the last three waking hours yields a larger isolated phase advance than increasing daytime light. These partial schedules were not observed HERA study arms.

Not additive: full minus the two partial contrasts = +0.2533 h. The full phase advance is smaller than the sum of the isolated advances in this default reconstruction.

These are averages across the six 48-hour assessment windows, with a 30-second output grid. Fixed τ and habitat-level sensitivities are in the executed notebook. At Level 2, dynamic daytime photopic light is lower than standard (80 vs 143 lux); daytime change alone delays the phase at τ = 24.2 h. Do not generalize the Level 1 attribution across physiology or locations.

Circadian phase and same-night latency are different outcomes. The stronger modeled evening phase response does not contradict the lack of predictive latency gain from prebed light in MeLiDos. No percentage causal attribution, sleep benefit or optimal habitat schedule is established.

Proposed H4: phase versus latency

If individual exposure records are available, late-wake light may add more predictive information for measured circadian phase than for same-night latency. Compare relative gains against endpoint-specific baselines; do not compare raw errors in hours and minutes.

The HERA article confirms environmental photometry and individual sleep/phase measurements, but personal exposure series must be confirmed with LSDA. Phase was measured in collection windows: interpolated daily phase is not an independent biomarker observation.

The separate-group anchoring reproduces mean inside/outside classification, but absolute errors are about 25 and 51 minutes. A close phase contrast does not validate absolute phase.

A common-offset alternative predicts SLS 5.5202 h and DLS 4.1911 h, both inside the window, missing the observed SLS category. Separate group offsets absorb premission differences and do not establish a universal x-minimum-to-aMT6s mapping.

Uncertainty without invented individual calibration

The notebook implements circular mean distance to target center and fraction of model days inside the window. A matched 24-draw Monte Carlo example samples τ and initial phase perturbation from explicitly assumed distributions for a hypothetical common starting phase. All 24 draws favored DLS by distance under those assumptions; this is not certainty, an individual posterior or an astronaut recommendation.

Published group means cannot identify individual τ, initial phase, marker offset and their covariance. Individual premission measurements and exposure are needed to calibrate those distributions. Modeled daily fractions are not measured daily biomarkers or the study's participant-day alignment percentages.

EMPIRICAL FINDINGS · EXPLORATORY · TWO EARTH COHORTS

Personal history first. Circadian alignment next.

We tested prospective sleep-latency prediction in two open, free-living cohorts: Manchester, UK (88 adults, 440 nights) and the analyzed MeLiDos subset (145 adults, 1,003 nights, 8 sites in 6 countries). Person-grouped development validation was followed by a held-out final eligible night per person, requiring at least three available nights.

History-based risk uses earlier long-latency nights, smoothed toward training prevalence. AUC measures discrimination, not percentage accuracy. These are analyzed subsets, not the entire MeLiDos project.

History was a strong baseline

History-based risk discriminated prolonged-latency nights in both holdouts. Population models did not demonstrate consistent improvement over personal history. Some had small numerical advantages on individual metrics.

Original H1 tested in MeLiDos

Adding mean light in the three hours before bed did not meaningfully improve prospective quantile-model latency prediction: holdout MAE was 12.87 versus 12.88 minutes without it. This does not show that evening light has no physiological effect.

Daytime light: exploratory signal

Greater daytime bright-light duration was associated with shorter median latency in the pooled quantile analysis: −2.81 min in Manchester and −1.07 min in MeLiDos per SD of the log-transformed exposure feature. Coefficients are descriptive, without confidence intervals or causal interpretation.

Comparison with NASA HERA

Across cohort summaries, shorter latency accompanies longer prior wakefulness. This is a descriptive comparison, not a pooled individual analysis. Published HERA dynamic lighting improved circadian alignment without a statistically significant latency difference. The quantitative benchmark above is already executed and compares phase proxies, not latency.

Working hypothesis: personal baseline and sleep pressure may dominate same-night latency prediction, while appropriately timed lighting may primarily support circadian alignment. None of these analyses validates predictions or benefits in astronauts.

Validation provenance and limits

Manchester development was exploratory. Its notebook reports one holdout evaluation with v3 models fixed, but no preceding seal commit. MeLiDos principal comparisons were specified before inspecting its outcomes. Cross-cohort transfer and pooled associations were explored after both holdouts were opened. Bootstrap intervals are descriptive and do not correct multiple comparisons.

History holdout MAE was 11.94 min (Manchester) and 12.13 min (MeLiDos). Manchester quantile_S was numerically lower at 11.51 min, with the paired difference interval spanning zero. Secondary MeLiDos LightGBM reached 12.05 min. These results do not support the absolute claim that no model ever beat history on any metric. Diary definitions and light sensors differ. Costa Rica/UCR was excluded from replication because its diaries had already been analyzed.
